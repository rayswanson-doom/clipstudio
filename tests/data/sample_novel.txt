The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter

Chapter 1: The Storm

Elena Marsh was a tall woman of thirty with black hair and grey eyes, and she had kept the lighthouse at Gull Point for six years. On the evening the storm arrived she was alone in the kitchen, stirring a pot of fish soup while the wind pulled at the shutters. The kitchen smelled of salt and smoke. She listened to the waves and tried not to think about her father, who had died the winter before.

A knock came at the door. Elena put down the spoon and crossed the stone floor. Outside, in the rain, stood a young man with a canvas bag and a face white with cold. He was perhaps twenty-five, with fair hair plastered to his forehead.

"Please," said the young man. "My boat has gone onto the rocks. I am Tomas Reed. I need shelter."

"Come in," said Elena, and she stepped back from the door. "You will catch your death out there."

Tomas stood dripping on the floor. He looked at the walls, at the old charts pinned beside the stove, at the brass lantern hanging from a hook. He had the quiet look of a man who had lost everything he owned in a single hour.

"Is there anyone else on the boat?" asked Elena.

"No," said Tomas. "Only me. I was carrying letters for the harbourmaster at Port Wren."

Elena gave him a blanket and a bowl of soup. The two of them sat by the stove while the storm struck the tower. The light above them turned slowly, throwing its beam across the black water. Every few minutes the whole house shook.

Later that night Elena climbed the long iron stairs to the top of the tower. The great lamp burned steadily. From the gallery she could see the wreck of Tomas's little boat, broken on the rocks below, and beyond it the white teeth of the waves. She stood there for a long while with the rain on her face.

* * *

By morning the wind had dropped. The sea was grey and quiet and the sun came up pale over the headland. Elena found Tomas on the beach, picking through the wreckage with a stick. A leather satchel lay half buried in the sand.

"The letters," said Tomas, and he knelt and lifted the satchel with both hands. "They are wet, but they are whole."

Elena looked at the satchel and then at the young man. "Port Wren is a day's walk along the cliff road," she said. "I will show you the way after we have eaten."

Chapter 2: The Road to Port Wren

They set out at noon. The cliff road wound above the sea between stone walls and fields of yellow gorse. Elena walked quickly, and Tomas followed with the satchel held against his chest. Neither of them spoke for the first hour.

"Why did you stay at the lighthouse?" asked Tomas at last. "A woman like you could live anywhere."

"My father kept the light before me," said Elena. "Someone must keep it burning. Besides, the sea is the only thing I have ever understood."

Tomas laughed softly. He told her about the town where he was born, about his mother, who sold bread in the market square, and about the long winters when the harbour froze. Elena listened. She found that she liked the sound of his voice.

In the afternoon they reached a small village with a church and a single street. An old woman named Margit Holt stood at the door of the inn, shading her eyes against the sun. She was a short, broad woman with white hair and a face like a winter apple.

"Elena Marsh," said Margit. "I did not think I would see you away from your tower."

"There was a wreck," said Elena. "This is Tomas Reed. He carries letters for the harbourmaster."

Margit looked at Tomas for a long moment. "Then he had better eat before he walks another step," she said. "Come inside, both of you. The fire is lit."

The inn was warm and crowded with fishermen. Margit set bread and cheese on the table and sat down across from them. She told them that the harbourmaster had died in the autumn, and that no one had been appointed in his place.

"Then who are the letters for?" asked Tomas.

"For the whole village," said Margit. "Half of them are waiting for news from the sea."

That evening Tomas stood on the village square with the satchel open and read out the names one by one. People came out of their houses with lanterns. A girl took her letter and ran home laughing. An old fisherman read his and sat down on the steps and wept. Elena watched from the shadow of the church, and for the first time in a year she felt the weight on her shoulders grow lighter.
